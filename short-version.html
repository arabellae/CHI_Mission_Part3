The Short Version

In One Minute

The Study
26 children aged 2 to 4 felt hidden shapes, sniffed lemon and vanilla, and picked names and feelings for them.
The Finding
Spiky shapes tended to go with “Kiki” and lemon, and round shapes with “Bouba” and vanilla. Lemon was called exciting and vanilla calm more often.
The Caution
These are tendencies from one small study, not rules. Shape-and-feeling links were not strong enough to count.

What the Researchers Wanted to Know

Adults tend to link senses in the same ways. Many people say a spiky shape sounds like “kiki” and a round shape sounds like “bouba.” Scientists call links like this cross-sensory correspondences. Most of this research has been done with older children and adults. Little was known about children aged 2 to 4, when the senses are developing quickly. This study asked: what links do preschoolers make between touch, smell and feelings, and how do they explain them?

What They Did

Twenty-six children, aged 2 to 4½, took part at their nurseries (daycares). Each child heard one story with four small tasks. A child felt three 3D shapes hidden in a box (Spiky, Round and Cylinder) and smelled three scents (lemon, vanilla and plain air). The child then matched them to made-up names (“Kiki” and “Bouba”), and to feelings (excited, calm, or neither).

What They Found

Some matches showed up more often than chance would predict. Spiky tended to go with “Kiki” and lemon. Round tended to go with “Bouba” and vanilla. The Cylinder tended to go with plain air. Lemon was called exciting more often, and vanilla was called calm more often. One link was not strong enough to count: shape and feeling. If anything, it leaned the opposite way from adults, but the researchers say it is only a trend.

Children mostly explained their choices with things they knew from life, for example calling the Round shape a “grape.” For smells, they often said how much they liked it (“I love it”) instead of describing it.

Key Points

What They Did

  • Cross-sensory correspondences are steady links between the senses, such as Bouba–Kiki.
  • Stories, characters, few choices and a familiar room helped with such young children.

What They Found

  • Preschoolers matched Spiky with “Kiki” and Round with “Bouba” using touch alone.
  • Children linked vanilla with calm and lemon with excited, as older children and adults do.
  • Spiky tended to go with lemon, Round with vanilla, and the Cylinder with plain air.
  • Shape-and-feeling matches were not statistically significant (a trend, not a finding).
  • The most common reasons were familiar things from everyday life.
  • Children described how shapes felt, but did not describe smells. Words for smells are hard to find.
  • Many children could not explain their choices. The links may work by instinct.

What It Could Mean

  • The authors suggest designers match cues to children’s links, not adults’. A lemon scent in a “calm” setting could confuse a child. This is a suggestion; it was not tested.
  • The authors say scent might help children who struggle to say how they feel. This was not tested.
