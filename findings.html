What the Children Found

Some pairings were picked more often than chance would predict. These are tendencies, not rules.

Reading the stats. “Significant” means a pattern unlikely to be chance (here, p below .05). A standardised residual above +2 means kids picked that pairing more than chance predicts; below −2, less.
Most Common Answer Was “Neither.” For every shape, most children’s most common name was neither “Kiki” nor “Bouba.” The patterns below are leans among the answers, not what most children said.

How Children Explained Their Choices

  • Touch: mostly familiar experience (42.5%) and how it felt (33.75%), from 80 reasons.
  • Smell: mostly familiar experience (69.6%) and liking or disliking it (19.6%), from 46 reasons. Nobody described what the smell was like.
  • The word “friend” came up when explaining feelings, for example Round “has friends” (excited), Spiky “has no friends” (calm).
  • Some children gave no reasons (3 for touch, 8 for smell).

Footnote on counts: 26 children took part, but the scent-and-feeling test uses 25 (one child skipped those questions). The paper is not fully consistent about this number.

Words like “matched” are used on purpose. These results show what children paired together, not that a smell causes a feeling.
